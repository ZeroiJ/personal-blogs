A 300-line key-value store taught me more than any database course

Sep 14, 2026·9 min read·written at an unreasonable hour

I've read the papers. I could explain LSM trees at a whiteboard. And yet the first time my own writes got acknowledged and then silently lost on restart, none of the papers prepared me for the specific flavor of panic that produces.

So this winter I built the dumbest durable key-value store I could stand: one append-only log, one in-memory index, reads served straight from mmap. Roughly 300 lines of Rust, zero crates beyond memmap2 and crc32fast. Here's what it got right, what it got embarrassingly wrong, and why I now actually get compaction.

The design I picked, and why

The goal wasn't performance. It was to feel every tradeoff in my hands. So: writes append to a log file, then fsync. Reads hit an HashMap<Vec<u8>, u64> that maps key → byte offset in the log. Simple enough that I could hold the whole thing in my head at 1am.

The moment a design fits on one index card, you stop being afraid of it. That fearlessness is worth more than the 20% you'd save with a fancier structure.

The log format

Each record is length-prefixed and checksummed — [keyLen|valLen|key|value|crc]. On open, we scan the file once and rebuild the offset map. A torn tail (crash mid-write) is detected by the CRC and simply truncated away.

// rebuild index on open — one linear scan
let mut off = 0usize;
while off < buf.len() {
    let rec = decode(&buf[off..])?;      // returns Err on bad CRC
    index.insert(rec.key, off as u64);
    off += rec.len;
}

That's the entire "recovery." It feels illegal coming from a Postgres background, which is exactly the point.

What the benchmarks actually said

Nothing surprising, which was itself the lesson — a dumb log is fast, because the OS is doing almost all of the work:

$ cargo bench
put   1.2 µs/op   (fsync dominates, surprise)
get   61 ns/op    (mmap hit, warm page)

Deletes were the first thing to crack: they're just another append (a tombstone), so the file grows forever. Which brings me to…

Why compaction exists (a confession)

I shipped it to my NAS, let it run for two weeks, and came back to a 40 MB file holding maybe 2 MB of live data. Every "overwrite" and "delete" was still in there. Compaction isn't an optimization — it's the tax you pay for the simplicity above, and I'd been treating it as optional garnish.

Part 2 will cover stop-the-world vs. level-by-level compaction, written while I implement the thing I skipped. If you want the code before it's respectable, it's on GitHub — mess and all.

TL;DR

  • An append log + HashMap gets you shockingly far — until deletes.
  • CRC on read saved me more than once; don't skip it because it feels paranoid.
  • If you can't explain why a component exists, you'll delete it and lose data. I nearly did.
