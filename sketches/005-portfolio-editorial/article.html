I could explain LSM trees at a whiteboard. I'd read the papers. And still, the first time my own acknowledged writes vanished on restart, none of it prepared me for the specific flavor of panic that produces.

So this is the story of adding a write-ahead log to RustDB — the toy B-tree engine I keep threatening to finish — what broke, what a CRC saved, and why I now treat compaction as load-bearing instead of garnish.

Why WAL, in one dumb sentence

Before: mutate pages in memory, flush whenever. Crash → those pages never hit disk → data lies about being saved. WAL flips it: write the intent to a log first, flush that, then touch pages at leisure. Replay the log on boot, you're whole again.

If a commit returns before the bytes are durable, you don't have a database — you have a suggestion.

The log format

Length-prefixed and checksummed: [lsn|len|payload|crc]. On open we scan the log once, stop at the first torn record, truncate there. That's the entire recovery path — suspiciously small, which is the point.

// rebuild from log — stop at first torn tail
let mut off = 0usize;
while off + HDR < buf.len() {
    let rec = decode(&buf[off..])?;   // Err on bad CRC → truncate here
    replay(&rec)?;
    off += rec.len;
}

What the numbers said

Nothing dramatic, which was itself the lesson — a log is fast because the OS is doing the heavy lifting:

$ cargo bench
wal_append   1.8 µs/op   (fsync dominates, as always)
page_read    61 ns/op    (mmap hit, warm page)

Deletes were the first thing to crack: they're just more log entries, so the file grows forever holding tombstones nobody needs.

What I'd tell past me

  • fsync before you say "committed" — no exceptions, no batching shortcuts at first.
  • CRC on read feels paranoid until the day it truncates a torn write and saves your week.
  • You will delete a component you don't understand. I nearly deleted the checkpoint step and lost two weeks of test data.
